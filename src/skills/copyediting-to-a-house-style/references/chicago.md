# chicago — The Chicago Manual of Style, 18th edition, with Merriam-Webster

US trade publishing. Spelling follows the first entry in *Merriam-Webster's
Collegiate Dictionary*; everything else follows the rules below, each stated
in this file's own words and carrying the ID a flag cites. The manual itself
is the authority where a case falls outside them, and a flag on such a case
says so.

## Punctuation

- **CMS-P1 Serial comma.** In a list of three or more, a comma goes before the
  conjunction: *bread, salt, and wine*.
- **CMS-P2 Em dash, closed.** An em dash (—) sets off a break or an aside with
  no space either side: *the gate—shut since spring—stood open*. Two hyphens or
  a spaced hyphen standing in for one is a departure.
- **CMS-P3 En dash for ranges.** Inclusive numbers and dates take an en dash
  (–): *pages 12–19*, *1990–94*. A range introduced by *from* or *between*
  takes words instead: *from 1990 to 1994*.
- **CMS-P4 Ellipsis.** Three spaced periods (. . .) for an omission or a trailing
  voice, with a fourth, closed up, where a sentence ends before it. A project
  style sheet may set the single character (…) instead; the rule is then
  consistency with that choice.
- **CMS-P5 Quotation marks.** Double marks, with single marks for a quotation
  inside one. Periods and commas go inside the closing mark; colons and
  semicolons go outside; question and exclamation marks go inside only when
  they belong to the quoted matter.
- **CMS-P6 Independent clauses.** Two independent clauses joined by *and*,
  *but*, *or*, *so* or *yet* take a comma before the conjunction unless both are
  short and closely tied. Two joined by a comma alone are a comma splice. In
  dialogue and close interior voice a splice the author chose is queried
  rather than corrected.
- **CMS-P7 Introductory phrases.** A comma after a long introductory phrase or
  a dependent clause; after a short one it may be omitted where no misreading
  results.
- **CMS-P8 Possessives.** Singular nouns take *'s*, names ending in *s*
  included: *Charles's*, *Kistáng's*. Plural nouns ending in *s* take the
  apostrophe alone.

## Dialogue

- **CMS-D1 New speaker, new paragraph.**
- **CMS-D2 Tags.** A quotation followed by its tag ends in a comma, question
  mark or exclamation mark inside the closing mark, and the tag begins
  lowercase: *"Go," she said.* An action beat that is not a speech verb is its
  own sentence: *"Go." She turned away.*
- **CMS-D3 Interruption and trailing off.** Speech cut off takes an em dash;
  speech that trails away takes an ellipsis (CMS-P4).

## Numbers

- **CMS-N1 Words or numerals.** Whole numbers from zero to one hundred, and
  round multiples of them (*three hundred*, *forty thousand*), are spelled out;
  other numbers take numerals. In fiction, numbers in dialogue are spelled out
  wherever that stays readable.
- **CMS-N2 Sentence openings.** A number beginning a sentence is spelled out,
  or the sentence is recast.
- **CMS-N3 Centuries and decades.** Centuries are spelled out in lowercase
  (*the twenty-first century*); decades take numerals with no apostrophe
  before the *s* (*the 1990s*).
- **CMS-N4 Times.** Times of day in running text are spelled out (*half past
  four*, *four o'clock*); where numerals are used, *a.m.* and *p.m.* are
  lowercase with periods.
- **CMS-N5 Dates.** Month, day, year — *November 20, 2026* — with a comma after
  the year when the sentence continues.
- **CMS-N6 Percentages.** *Percent* is spelled out in running text.

## Capitals

- **CMS-C1 Titles of people.** A title is capitalized directly before a name
  (*Captain Ardo*) and lowercase when it stands alone or follows the name (*the
  captain*, *Ardo, captain of the guard*).
- **CMS-C2 Kinship terms.** Capitalized when used as a name or in address
  (*"Ask Mother"*), lowercase with a possessive (*my mother*).
- **CMS-C3 Titles of works.** Headline style: capitalize the first and last
  words and every word but articles, coordinating conjunctions, *to*, and
  prepositions.

## Italics

- **CMS-I1 Titles of long works.** Books, films, periodicals, long poems and
  ships take italics; chapters, short stories and songs take quotation marks.
- **CMS-I2 Foreign words.** A word from another language that readers will not
  know is italicized; one in *Merriam-Webster* is set roman. Words of a
  project's invented languages follow the project style sheet.
- **CMS-I3 Emphasis.** Italics, used sparingly; never bold or capitals in
  running prose.

## Hyphenation and spelling

- **CMS-H1 Compound modifiers.** A compound before a noun is hyphenated (*a
  well-kept secret*); after the noun it usually is not (*the secret was well
  kept*). A compound with an adverb ending in *-ly* is never hyphenated.
- **CMS-H2 Prefixes.** Prefixes close up (*nonessential*, *preexisting*,
  *reelect*) except before a capital, a numeral, or where a doubled letter or
  another word would misread (*anti-intellectual*, *re-cover* for cover again).
- **CMS-H3 Spelling.** The first spelling *Merriam-Webster* gives: *toward*,
  *gray*, *judgment*, *traveled*.
- **CMS-H4 Abbreviations.** *US* and *UK* without periods; *Mr.*, *Mrs.*,
  *Dr.* with them.

## Word choice

- **CMS-W1 *That* and *which*.** *That* introduces a restrictive clause, *which*
  with commas a nonrestrictive one. In dialogue and voice, a restrictive
  *which* is left as written.
